A good night routine is a calm, repeatable set of steps that starts one to two hours before bed: dim the lights, put screens away, plan tomorrow, and do something quiet until you feel sleepy. Pair it with a consistent sleep window and an early caffeine cutoff, and both your sleep and your mornings get easier. Here is a wind-down timeline you can copy, sample bedtime routines, and what to do on nights you can't sleep.

## Key takeaways

- Fix your wake time first, then count back 7 to 9 hours to set your bedtime.
- Start winding down about two hours before bed, in stages, not all at once.
- Dim lights and park your phone outside the bedroom for the last 30 to 60 minutes.
- Stop caffeine by early afternoon. It can keep working for hours.
- Spend five minutes planning tomorrow so worries don't follow you into bed.
- If you can't sleep, get up and do something calm instead of lying there.

## Why a night routine matters

Sleep is the base for almost every other habit. When you sleep well, it is easier to work out, eat sensibly, focus and stay patient. When you don't, even small habits feel hard.

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends that adults get at least 7 hours of sleep a night on a regular basis. Many people get less, not because they can't sleep, but because the evening drifts: one more episode, one more reel, one more message.

A night routine fixes the drift. It tells your brain, through repetition, that the day is ending. Over time the routine itself becomes a cue for sleepiness.

It also sets up the next day. Clothes laid out, bag packed and one task written down means your [morning routine](/blog/morning-routine) starts smoothly instead of in a rush.

## Set a consistent sleep window first

Before you add any steps, decide when you will sleep and wake. A consistent window does more for most people than any single bedtime trick.

1. Pick your wake time based on your day (work, college, school run).
2. Count back 7 to 9 hours. That is your target bedtime range.
3. Keep both times within about an hour on weekends too.

If you sleep at 1:30 AM and want to sleep at 11:00, don't try to jump straight there. Move bedtime and wake time about 15 minutes earlier every few nights. Getting daylight soon after waking helps, because it nudges your body clock earlier. Long or late-afternoon naps work against you, so keep naps short and early if you take them.

This is the honest answer to "how to sleep early": shift slowly, protect your wake time, and let tiredness build up at the right hour.

## The wind-down timeline

Think of the evening as a slope, not a switch. Here is a timeline that works for a 10:30 PM bedtime. Shift it to match yours.

### 2 hours before bed (around 8:30 PM)

- Finish dinner, or at least the heavy part of it. Big meals right before bed can make it harder to settle.
- Wrap up demanding work and intense exercise. A gentle walk after dinner is fine.
- Start lowering the lights at home. Switch from bright overhead lights to lamps.

### 1 hour before bed (around 9:30 PM)

- Put your phone on charge outside the bedroom, or at least across the room.
- Plan tomorrow in five minutes (more on this below).
- Do your "closing" chores: lay out clothes, fill a water bottle, pack your bag.
- Take a warm shower or wash up. Many people find a warm shower relaxing in the evening.

### 30 minutes before bed (around 10:00 PM)

- Keep the lights low and the room cool and quiet.
- Read a paper book, journal, stretch gently, or do a few minutes of slow breathing.
- Skip anything that gets you thinking hard: work email, arguments, news.

### Lights out (10:30 PM)

Go to bed when you feel sleepy, close to the same time each night. If you are not sleepy yet, give it a little longer with your book rather than trying to force it.

## Screens and light

Light is one of the main signals your body uses to tell day from night. Bright light in the evening, including from screens and overhead bulbs, can delay the point where you start to feel sleepy.

Screens have a second problem: the content. A reel, a game or a work chat keeps your brain engaged and makes it easy to lose track of time. Night mode and lower brightness help a little, but they don't make doomscrolling restful.

Practical fixes:

- Charge your phone outside the bedroom and use a basic alarm clock, or keep the phone face down across the room.
- Set a "screens off" time an hour before bed. If that feels hard, start with 15 minutes and build up.
- Replace the scroll with something you enjoy: a novel, music, a puzzle, talking to someone at home.
- Turn bright overhead lights off in the last hour and use a lamp.

## Caffeine timing

Caffeine blocks the chemical signal that builds sleep pressure during the day, and its effects can last for several hours. How long varies a lot from person to person, so a 4 PM coffee can be fine for one friend and ruin your night.

A simple rule: stop caffeine by early afternoon, around 8 hours before bed, and see how you sleep for a week. Remember it is not just coffee. Chai, green tea, cola, energy drinks and some chocolate all contain caffeine.

Alcohol is worth a mention too. It can make you feel drowsy, but it tends to disrupt sleep later in the night, so a nightcap is not a sleep aid.

## Plan tomorrow in five minutes

A racing mind is one of the most common reasons people lie awake. Planning tomorrow before bed gets the open loops out of your head and onto paper.

There is some research behind this. In a small sleep-lab study, Scullin and colleagues (2018, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General) found that young adults who spent five minutes writing a to-do list for the coming days fell asleep faster than those who wrote about tasks they had already completed.

Try this:

1. Write the three things you need to do tomorrow.
2. Circle the one that matters most.
3. Write the very first step for it ("open the slides", "call the bank at 10").

Then close the notebook. The plan is safe, and you don't need to rehearse it in bed.

## Journaling before bed

Journaling is different from planning. Planning looks forward; journaling helps you process the day. You don't need a fancy notebook or long entries. Three short prompts are enough:

- **One thing that went well today.** It can be tiny: a good lunch, a kind message.
- **What is on my mind right now?** Write it down so it doesn't keep circling.
- **What can wait until tomorrow?** Give yourself permission to stop.

If you prefer, use a "worry dump": set a timer for five minutes and write every worry, big or small, without fixing anything. Many people find it easier to let go once the thoughts are on paper.

| Routine | Steps (in order) | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| 15-minute | Phone on charge outside room, lay out clothes, wash up, 5 min reading in bed | Busy evenings, beginners |
| 30-minute | Dim lights, plan tomorrow (5 min), shower, journal 3 lines, read until sleepy | Most adults on workdays |
| 60-minute | Screens off, tidy for 10 min, plan tomorrow, warm shower, stretch, read or breathing exercise | People who find it hard to switch off |
| Student / exam | Stop studying 45 min before bed, pack bag, quick review of 5 flashcards, shower, no phone, lights out on time | Exam season, early classes |
| Late shift / night owl | Same steps, shifted later: dim lights 1 hour before your sleep time, blackout curtains, phone away, fixed wake time | People who can't sleep early because of work |

## What to do if you can't sleep

Even with a good routine, some nights you will lie awake. A few things help:

- **Don't fight it in bed.** If you have been awake for what feels like about 20 minutes, get up, go somewhere dim and do something calm and a bit boring until you feel sleepy. Then go back to bed.
- **Hide the clock.** Checking the time and counting the hours left tends to make anxiety worse.
- **Stay off your phone.** Bright light and engaging content are the opposite of what you need.
- **Keep your wake time.** Sleeping in after a bad night can make the next night harder. Get up at your usual time and go to bed when sleepy.
- **Skip the late nap.** If you must nap, keep it short and before mid-afternoon.

If you have trouble sleeping most nights for several weeks, or you feel exhausted during the day, snore loudly or wake up gasping, please see a doctor. Persistent insomnia is common and treatable, and cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is a well-established option a doctor can point you to. A routine helps, but it is not a substitute for proper care.

## Common night routine mistakes

**Starting at bedtime.** If you scroll until 11:59 and then begin your routine, it won't work. The wind-down starts an hour or two earlier.

**Revenge bedtime procrastination.** Staying up late to grab "me time" after a busy day is very common. Build some of that time into your evening routine instead, like reading or a hobby, so you don't have to steal it from sleep.

**Using the bed for everything.** Working, eating and scrolling in bed weakens the link between bed and sleep. Keep the bed mostly for sleep.

**Huge weekend swings.** Sleeping at 3 AM on Saturday and waking at noon makes Sunday night and Monday morning much harder.

## Start tonight

You don't need to overhaul your evenings. Tonight, do three things: set your phone to charge outside the bedroom, write tomorrow's top task, and go to bed at the time you picked. Tomorrow, do the same. Add a step only once those feel easy.

A good night routine is quiet and a little boring, and that's what makes it work. Keep it consistent for a few weeks and falling asleep, and getting up, will likely feel easier.
