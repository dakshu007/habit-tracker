There's no magic number. The best-known study on the question found that it took a median of about 66 days for a new daily behaviour to become automatic, but individual estimates ranged from 18 to 254 days. The popular "21 days" figure grew out of a 1960 self-help book, not a habit study. How long it takes you depends mostly on how simple the habit is and how consistently you tie it to the same cue.

## Key takeaways

- **The 21-day rule isn't based on habit research.** It's a shortened version of a surgeon's observation from 1960.
- **About 66 days is a better rough guide,** but treat it as a middle figure, not a deadline. Some habits take weeks, others many months.
- **Simple, daily habits with a fixed cue form fastest.** Drinking water after breakfast becomes automatic sooner than going to the gym.
- **Missing one day doesn't reset you.** Missing several in a row can.
- **Plan for about two months, in phases,** and judge progress by how automatic the habit feels, not by the calendar.

## Where the 21-day idea came from

In 1960, plastic surgeon Maxwell Maltz published *Psycho-Cybernetics*. He had noticed that patients seemed to need around three weeks to get used to a change, such as seeing a new face in the mirror after surgery. He wrote that it takes "a minimum of about 21 days" for an old mental image to dissolve and a new one to settle in.

The book sold widely and the idea spread. Over the years, "a minimum of about 21 days" was shortened to "it takes 21 days to form a habit". The word "minimum" got lost, and an observation about adjusting to change turned into a rule about building habits.

### What about the 21/90 rule?

The 21/90 rule is a newer twist: 21 days to build a habit, 90 days to make it part of your lifestyle. There's no research behind those specific numbers. Still, it gets one thing right. Three weeks is a start, and a habit needs months of repetition before it truly runs on its own.

Short challenges can still be useful. A defined window is easier to commit to than "forever". Just treat day 21 as a checkpoint, not a finish line.

## What the research actually says

### The 66-day study

The study most people mean when they say "66 days" is by Phillippa Lally, Cornelia van Jaarsveld, Henry Potts and Jane Wardle at University College London. It was published online in 2009 and in print in the *European Journal of Social Psychology* in 2010.

96 volunteers each chose one eating, drinking or activity behaviour to do daily in the same context (for example, after breakfast). For 12 weeks they reported whether they did it and rated how automatic it felt.

What they found:

- **Automaticity rose fastest at the start, then levelled off.** Early repetitions did the most work, and later ones added less and less.
- **The median was 66 days.** For participants whose data fitted the model well, that's how long it took to reach 95% of their peak automaticity.
- **The range was huge: 18 to 254 days.** Some people got there in under three weeks, others were projected to need most of a year.
- **Missing one opportunity didn't materially affect the process.** One skipped day didn't knock people back to the start.

Two caveats are worth knowing. The 66-day median comes from the subset of participants whose data fitted the curve well, not from all 96. And the study ran for 12 weeks (84 days), so longer estimates like 254 days were projections from each person's curve, not observed results.

### Gym habits vs handwashing

A 2023 study in *PNAS* by Anastasia Buyalskaya and colleagues (including Katherine Milkman, Angela Duckworth and Colin Camerer) took a different approach. Instead of asking people how automatic a habit felt, they used machine learning to measure how predictable behaviour became over time. They looked at two large real-world datasets: gym check-ins from more than 30,000 gym members, and handwashing data from over 3,000 hospital workers.

Their conclusion: there's no magic number. Gym habits typically took months to form, with a median of roughly four to seven months. Handwashing habits formed in a couple of weeks. The researchers suggest the gap comes down to frequency and complexity. Handwashing is a short, simple action repeated many times a shift, while gym visits happen less often and involve more steps.

One more detail stands out. For many gym-goers, going on the same days of the week as before was one of the strongest predictors of going again. Consistent timing helps.

### What "formed" actually means

Researchers measure habit as automaticity: how much you do something without having to think or decide. A formed habit doesn't mean you'll never skip it or that it's always effortless. It means the cue starts the behaviour more and more on its own.

That's why the calendar is a poor judge. A better test is how the habit feels. Do you start without deciding to? Would it feel odd to skip? If yes, it's well on its way, whether that's day 30 or day 100.

## What makes a habit form faster

You can't control everything, but you can stack the odds in your favour:

- **Keep it simple.** A short, single action forms faster than a complex chain. "Two minutes of stretching" beats "a full workout" as a starting habit.
- **Use one consistent cue.** Tie the habit to the same trigger every time: after breakfast, after you park, after you brush your teeth.
- **Same time, same place.** Context is what your brain links to the behaviour. Changing it every day slows that link down.
- **Do it often.** A daily habit gets more repetitions in a week than a three-times-a-week habit, so it usually becomes automatic sooner.
- **Make it rewarding.** A small, immediate payoff, like ticking it off or enjoying a podcast during your walk, makes you want to repeat it.
- **Make it easy to start.** Lay out what you need in advance so there's nothing to decide in the moment.

## Does missing a day reset the clock?

No. This is one of the most useful findings from the Lally study: missing a single opportunity did not materially affect the habit-forming process.

What does hurt is a run of misses, because then you're repeating a different pattern (not doing it). So the practical rule is never miss twice. If you skip a day, do a tiny version the next day and carry on.

## A practical 66-day plan

66 days isn't a guarantee, but it's a sensible window to plan around. Here's how to break it into phases.

| Phase | Days | Goal | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Start tiny | 1 to 7 | Show up daily | Shrink the habit to two minutes and pick one cue |
| 2. Lock in the cue | 8 to 21 | Same time, same place | Fix the trigger, set a reminder, prepare the night before |
| 3. Get through the dip | 22 to 45 | Keep the chain going | Expect motivation to drop, never miss twice, review weekly |
| 4. Grow and test | 46 to 66 | Build the full version | Increase slowly and check how automatic it feels |

### Days 1 to 7: Start tiny

Choose one habit and make it almost too easy. Read one page, do five squats, write one sentence. Write it as a plan: "After I [cue], I will [habit]." The only goal this week is to do it every day.

### Days 8 to 21: Lock in the cue

Keep the habit small and focus on consistency. Do it at the same time and in the same place every day. Set a [reminder](/reminders) for the first few weeks, and remove friction: book on the pillow, shoes by the door.

If you like structure, this is where a 21-day challenge fits well. You can invite a friend with a code so you both start on the same day.

### Days 22 to 45: Get through the dip

This is where most habits fade. The novelty is gone and the habit isn't automatic yet. Expect it. Don't add difficulty here; just protect the streak. Use never miss twice, and spend five minutes each Sunday asking what got in the way and what you'll change next week.

### Days 46 to 66: Grow and test

Now start increasing slowly: more pages, more minutes. Hold each new level for a week before raising it again. Around day 66, check in honestly. Do you start without thinking? Would skipping feel strange? If yes, great. If not, keep going. Some habits need longer, and that's normal.

## How to tell your habit has formed

Use these signs instead of a date:

- You start it without a reminder.
- It feels odd or incomplete to skip it.
- You don't negotiate with yourself about whether to do it.
- It survives a disrupted day, like travel or a late night, at least in a small form.

If two or three of these are true, the habit is becoming automatic. If not, look at the cue and the size. Usually the habit is too big or the trigger is too vague.

## The bottom line

Forget 21 days. Plan for about two months, expect the timing to vary, and focus on what speeds it up: a simple action, a fixed cue, the same time and place, and a small reward. Miss a day if you must, but never two in a row.
