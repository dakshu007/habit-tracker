To stop procrastinating, make starting feel smaller and less unpleasant than avoiding. Shrink the task to a 5-minute first step, put your phone out of reach and set a timer. Procrastination is usually not laziness: it's a way of escaping a bad feeling (boredom, anxiety, self-doubt) that the task stirs up, so the fixes that work target the feeling, not your character.

Below: why it happens, how to spot your type, 14 techniques that help, and a plan for exam season.

## Key takeaways

- Procrastination is mostly about avoiding unpleasant feelings, not about laziness or bad time management.
- The hardest part is starting. Shrink the first step until it feels almost too easy.
- Different types of procrastination need different fixes (see the table below).
- Timers, if-then plans and fewer distractions make starting more automatic.
- Being kind to yourself after a slip tends to reduce future procrastination. Beating yourself up tends to feed it.
- Motivation often follows action, so build the habit of starting.

## Why you procrastinate (it's not laziness)

Lazy people are happy doing nothing. Procrastinators usually do want to finish, and feel bad the whole time they avoid it. That's the clue.

Psychologists Fuschia Sirois and Tim Pychyl have argued that procrastination is largely about short-term mood repair (Sirois and Pychyl, 2013, *Social and Personality Psychology Compass*). When a task makes you feel bored, anxious, confused or not good enough, avoiding it brings instant relief. You open Instagram, the bad feeling fades, and your brain learns that avoidance works. Your future self pays the bill.

This is why "just try harder" rarely helps. The issue isn't effort. The task feels threatening in some way, and your brain prefers comfort now over a reward later.

It also explains the guilt loop. You delay, feel guilty, the guilt makes the task feel even worse, so you delay more. Stopping procrastination means breaking that loop.

Tasks feel most aversive when they are:

- Boring or frustrating
- Vague, with no obvious place to start
- Hard, or likely to show you're not perfect
- Not meaningful to you personally
- Rewarded only far in the future

## What type of procrastinator are you?

| Type | What it sounds like | What's really going on | Fixes to try first |
|---|---|---|---|
| Perfectionist | "I'll start when I can do it properly." | Fear of doing it badly | Ugly first draft, timebox, define "done" |
| Overwhelmed | "There's too much. Where do I even begin?" | Task feels huge and vague | Tiny next action, plan tomorrow tonight |
| Bored | "This is so dull." | No interest or meaning | Temptation bundling, Pomodoro, small rewards |
| Distracted | "I'll just check my phone first." | Easy escapes within reach | Phone in another room, blockers, focus timer |
| Anxious | "What if I fail?" | Fear of judgement or results | 5-minute start, self-compassion, a buddy |
| Deadline-driven | "I work better under pressure." | Only urgency creates action | Self-set mini deadlines, accountability partner |

Most people are a mix. Pick the row that feels most familiar and start with those fixes.

## 14 techniques to stop procrastinating

### 1. Use the 5-minute start

Tell yourself you'll work for just 5 minutes, and that you're allowed to stop after. Starting is usually the most painful part; once you're moving, the dread often shrinks. If you do stop after 5 minutes, that's fine. It's 5 minutes more than you'd have done otherwise.

### 2. Find the tiny next action

"Write assignment" is not a task, it's a project. The next action might be "open a doc and type the title" or "find two sources". Make the first step so concrete you can picture yourself doing it. Vague tasks feel threatening. Specific ones feel doable.

### 3. Name the feeling

Before you open YouTube, pause and ask: what am I trying to avoid right now? Boredom? A bad mark? Confusion? Naming the feeling makes it smaller and points to the right fix. Confused? Ask someone. Bored? Add music. Scared? Do the 5-minute start.

### 4. Timebox it with the Pomodoro Technique

The Pomodoro Technique was created by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s and named after his tomato-shaped kitchen timer. Work for 25 minutes, take a 5-minute break, and after four rounds take a longer one. A fixed end point makes a hard task feel finite.

Habit Tracker's [focus timer](/focus-timer) defaults to 25 minutes, offers ambient sounds such as rain, ocean and brown noise, and logs the minutes to a habit when the session ends. So "study 50 minutes a day" can be two focus sessions that fill themselves in.

### 5. Write an implementation intention

An implementation intention is an if-then plan: "If it's 7 PM on a weekday, then I sit at my desk and open my notes." A 2006 meta-analysis by Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran found that if-then plans like these had a medium-to-large effect on whether people reached their goals. The decision is made in advance, so there's nothing to negotiate in the moment. Pair it with a [reminder](/reminders) at that exact time.

### 6. Remove distractions before you start

Willpower is a weak defence against a phone in your hand. Put it in another room or a drawer, close extra browser tabs, and block your worst time-sink sites while you work. Make distractions take effort to reach. If your phone habit is the main problem, a [dopamine detox](/blog/dopamine-detox) can help you reset it.

### 7. Bundle the task with something you enjoy

Temptation bundling pairs a task you avoid with something you like. Katherine Milkman, Julia Minson and Kevin Volpp tested this in a 2014 study by giving people gripping audiobooks they could only listen to at the gym, which increased gym visits. Try only drinking your favourite cold coffee while doing admin, or saving a playlist for cleaning. We explain the idea in our [Atomic Habits summary](/blog/atomic-habits-summary) too.

### 8. Plan tomorrow tonight

Spend five minutes before bed writing tomorrow's top three tasks and the first action for each. In the morning you skip the "what should I do?" phase, which is where a lot of procrastination hides. It fits neatly into a [night routine](/blog/night-routine).

### 9. Eat the frog, or start with the easiest task

"Eat the frog", popularised by Brian Tracy's book *Eat That Frog!*, means doing your hardest, most important task first, before the day drains your energy. It suits morning people and clearly defined big tasks.

The opposite approach, easiest-first, builds momentum: a quick win makes the next task feel lighter. It suits days when you feel overwhelmed or low on energy.

Neither is right for everyone. A useful rule: if dread is your problem, eat the frog so it stops hanging over you. If inertia is your problem, start easy to get moving, then go straight to the important task.

### 10. Lower the bar for the first draft

Perfectionists delay because the first attempt has to be good. Give yourself permission to make it bad. Write an ugly draft, decide that version one is for nobody's eyes, and define what "done" looks like before you begin. You can't edit a blank page.

### 11. Get an accountability partner

Telling someone your plan and checking in daily adds a small social cost to skipping. Pick a friend, sibling or classmate and agree on what you'll report. Habit Tracker's [accountability buddy](/accountability-buddy) feature sends your daily check-in (what's done and your longest streak) to a friend on WhatsApp in one tap.

### 12. Set your own deadlines

A project due in four weeks feels like it's due "later", so later is when it gets done. Break it into weekly milestones with their own dates, and share them with someone if you can. Smaller, closer deadlines swap one big panic for steady progress.

### 13. Forgive yourself after a slip

This sounds soft, but it's practical. A 2010 study by Michael Wohl, Tim Pychyl and Shannon Bennett followed first-year university students across two midterm exams. Students who forgave themselves for procrastinating before the first exam procrastinated less before the second, and the link ran through feeling less negative about it. Guilt keeps a task feeling unpleasant. Self-compassion makes it easier to approach again.

So when you lose an afternoon, tell yourself: "That happened. It's normal. What's the next small step?" Then take it.

### 14. Make starting a habit

The long-term fix is to remove the daily decision. If "focus session at 9 AM" is a habit, you don't debate it each morning. Track it like any other habit and protect the streak. ## How to stop procrastinating as a student during exams

Exam season makes everything harder: the stakes are high, the syllabus is huge and your phone is right there. A simple plan:

- **Map the syllabus once.** List every chapter or topic on one page. Seeing it all shrinks the overwhelm.
- **Split it into daily targets.** Divide the topics by the days left, with a buffer day each week for revision.
- **Study in Pomodoros.** Four 25-minute sessions with breaks is a solid morning. Count sessions, not hours.
- **Phone out of the room.** Use a basic alarm clock or a timer if you need one.
- **Test yourself.** Close the book and write what you remember, or solve past papers. It feels harder than rereading, which is part of why it helps.
- **Check in with a friend.** A quick "I did three sessions, you?" message keeps you both honest without an hour of chatting.
- **Protect your sleep.** An all-nighter trades tomorrow's focus for tonight's guilt relief.

If you've already lost a few days, don't try to make it all up in one heroic night. Restart with today's target.

## How to stay motivated, and what self-discipline really is

Motivation is unreliable. It shows up after you start more often than before. That's why most of the techniques above focus on starting rather than on feeling ready.

Self-discipline, in practice, is less about gritting your teeth and more about setup: fewer distractions, clear next steps, fixed times and habits that run without debate. People who look disciplined have often just made the right action the easy one.

A few ways to keep going once you've started:

- Track small wins so you can see progress building.
- Reward yourself right after a hard session, not at the end of the month.
- Connect the task to something you care about: "this exam gets me closer to the job I want."

## When procrastination might be something more

If procrastination comes with constant low mood, trouble focusing on almost anything, or a sense that it's completely out of your control, it may be linked to something like depression, anxiety or ADHD. These are common and treatable. Talking to a doctor or counsellor is a sensible step, not a sign of weakness.

## The bottom line

You don't need to become a different person to stop procrastinating. You need to make the first step smaller, the distractions harder to reach and the act of starting a habit. Pick two techniques from this list, the ones that match your type, and try them for a week.
